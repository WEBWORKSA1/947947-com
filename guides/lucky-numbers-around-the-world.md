Most number beliefs come from one of three places: **sound** (a number sounds like a lucky or unlucky word), **scripture and ritual** (a number appears in sacred texts or ceremonies) or **shape and history** (a number looks or once meant something). The same digit can carry opposite meanings in different places, which is why international brands and travellers pay attention.

## East Asia: numbers you can hear

In Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese and Korean, the word for four sounds like the word for death. The result, called tetraphobia, is visible in daily life: lifts that label the fourth floor "F", hospitals without room 4, and phone numbers priced by how many 4s and 8s they contain. Read more in our guides to [Chinese lucky numbers]({{ r }}guides/chinese-lucky-numbers/) and [Japanese number wordplay]({{ r }}guides/japanese-number-wordplay/).

Korea adds its own twists. Ages ending in 9 are traditionally considered risky years, and texting codes such as 8282 (*ppalli ppalli*, hurry) and 486 (I love you) turn numbers into words.

## South and Southeast Asia

In Thailand, nine (*gâo*) sounds like the word for stepping forward, so it is a favourite for weddings, shop openings and phone numbers. Thai internet users also write 555 for laughter, because five is pronounced *hâa*.

In India, numerology often follows the Vedic system, which links each number to a planet: 1 to the Sun, 9 to Mars, 4 to Rahu and so on. Many families consult it when naming a child, choosing a mobile number or setting a launch date. The number 108 is sacred in Hinduism and Buddhism and appears in prayer beads.

## The Jewish and Muslim worlds

In Hebrew, letters double as numbers. The two letters that spell *chai*, life, add up to 18, so gifts are often given in multiples of 18. In South Asian Muslim communities, 786 is written as a shorthand for *Bismillah*, because that is the total value of its letters in the traditional Arabic letter-number system.

## Europe and the West

Seven is the classic lucky number of the West, from the seven days of creation to the "lucky seven" of dice and slot machines. Thirteen is the classic unlucky one, so some buildings skip a 13th floor and some airlines skip row 13. Italians avoid 17 instead, because the Roman numeral XVII can be rearranged into VIXI, "I have lived", an epitaph. In Russia and much of Eastern Europe, flowers are given in odd numbers, because even numbers are reserved for funerals.

## What this means for you

If you are choosing a number that others will see, such as a phone number, a price, a room or a launch date, think about who will read it. Avoiding the strongest negatives costs nothing.
