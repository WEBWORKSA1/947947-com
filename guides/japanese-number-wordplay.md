Japanese has several ways to read each digit: the Chinese-derived readings (ichi, ni, san), native Japanese readings (hito, futa, mi) and even English ones (wan, tsū). Goroawase (語呂合わせ) picks whichever syllables make a word. The result is a playful code used in phone numbers, adverts, passwords, history lessons and text messages.

## Remembering history

Japanese students memorise dates with rhymes. The capital moved to Heian-kyō, today's Kyoto, in 794: *naku yo uguisu Heian-kyō*, "the nightingale sings, Heian-kyō". The traditional date for the Kamakura shogunate, 1192, becomes *ii kuni tsukurō*, "let's build a good country".

## Days named after numbers

Goroawase also creates commemorative days. 9 and 4 read *ku-shi*, the word for comb, so 4 September has been Comb Day since 1978. 7 and 10 read *nat-tō*, so 10 July is Nattō Day. Tokyo Skytree is 634 metres tall partly because 6-3-4 reads *Mu-sa-shi*, the old name of the Tokyo region.

## Why 4 and 9 are avoided

The same wordplay has a dark side. 4 read as *shi* sounds like death (死), and 9 read as *ku* sounds like suffering (苦). Some hospitals and hotels skip room numbers with 4 or 9, gifts are not given in sets of four, and 49 (*shi-ku*) is avoided on number plates. Seven and eight, by contrast, are lucky: the Seven Lucky Gods are a popular theme, and the character for 8 widens toward the bottom, a symbol of growing prosperity.
